Congress agreed 417–3 on data centers. What else do they agree on?

Created on November 01, 2026

2026   ·   data-science   ·   project

Democrats and Republicans split on most energy votes this term. Then, on September 16, they voted 417 to 3 on one. Here’s what the House actually agrees on, who crosses party lines, and how to look up your own representative before Tuesday.

Energy is one of the most divided subjects in the House. Of the 29 recorded votes on energy bills in this Congress, 83% split along party lines. In September 2025, the GRID Power Act, which tells federal regulators to put “dispatchable” power plants (such as fossil-fuel plants) at the front of the line to connect to the grid, passed 216–206. 2% of Democrats and 99% of Republicans voted yes.

Then came the Ratepayer Protection Act. It asks every state to consider a rule that makes very large data centers (those drawing 100 megawatts or more) pay the full cost of the power lines and upgrades built to serve them, so that cost is recovered from the data center rather than from other customers. It passed 417–3.

That made me want to know what else Congress agrees on. The answer is more than the headlines suggest.

I took every recorded vote in the House since January 2025 (674 of them) and the official topic the Library of Congress assigns to each bill. Here’s what the record shows.

Nearly 4 in 10 votes on bills are bipartisan

Across 420 votes on bills (final passage, fast-tracked bills and amendments), 38% were bipartisan: the share of Democrats and the share of Republicans voting yes were within 25 points of each other. 43% were party-line: the parties were more than 80 points apart. The rest fell in between.

How a bill comes to the floor matters more than its subject:

  • Fast-tracked bills, which need a two-thirds majority to pass, were bipartisan 87% of the time.
  • Regular bills on final passage were party-line 80% of the time. These are the big, contested bills that make the news.

And these counts understate the agreement. Many bills pass by voice vote, with no recorded roll call, and those are almost always uncontroversial.

Where the parties agree, and where they split

How far apart the two parties voted, by issue. Each bar is the typical gap between the share of Democrats and the share of Republicans voting yes.
  • Among the most divided: energy, environmental protection, immigration, labor, and armed forces and national security. On these, the typical vote had the two parties 84 to 95 points apart.
  • Most bipartisan: science and technology, transportation, emergency management, commerce and international affairs. Here the typical vote had the parties within 10 points of each other.

One caution on taxes. The “Taxation” bar looks fully bipartisan because its seven votes were small, specific bills, such as disaster filing extensions and help for fraud victims. The big 2025 tax-and-spending law (H.R. 1) is classified under “Economics and Public Finance”. Both of its votes were strictly party-line: 0% of Democrats and 99% of Republicans voted yes.

Who crosses party lines?

On the 317 votes where most Democrats and most Republicans voted opposite ways, the typical member sided with the other party about 2% of the time. That’s true in both parties: the median is 2.3% for Democrats and 2.5% for Republicans. Only 41 of the 445 voting members crossed over more than 10% of the time.

There are two different kinds of crossing in that list:

  • Most cross from the middle. Nearly all of these members belong to the Problem Solvers Caucus or the Blue Dog Coalition, and they vote yes on almost every bill both parties support (98–100%).
  • Massie crosses from the edge. He votes yes on only 56% of the bills both parties back, against 99% for the typical Republican. The members who cross from the middle sit at 98–100%.

My previous post found that this second pattern, voting no on bills both parties back, is the House’s second dimension. It is also what sets the Freedom Caucus apart.

Crossing party lines isn’t good or bad in itself. It can reflect a district, a caucus, a principle or a deal. These numbers describe what happened, not why.

Look up your own representative

How your representative votes is a free, non-partisan dashboard built from the same data. It works the same way for all 445 voting members. Pick your state and representative to see:

  • how often they voted, and how often they sided with the other party;
  • where they sit among every other member;
  • their recent votes on bills, each linked to the bill on Congress.gov, with a filter by issue.

Don’t know who your representative is? Enter your address at house.gov. You can also see every member on a map of the House.

Then vote

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3. Every House seat is on the ballot. You can check your registration, find your polling place and see what’s on your ballot at vote.gov.

The record is public so that voters can use it. Whoever you support, look at what your representative did, then go vote.

Methods

Data.

  • Votes: Voteview (Lewis, Poole, Rosenthal, Boche, Rudkin & Sonnet). The files were downloaded on 2026-10-17 and cover the 119th House’s 674 recorded votes, from January 3, 2025 to September 16, 2026.
  • Topics: the Congress.gov API (Library of Congress). The Congressional Research Service assigns every bill one policy area, plus subject terms and a summary. All 429 bills that received a roll call were found.
  • Senate votes, voice votes and committee votes aren’t included.

Definitions. Everything is computed directly from the votes, with no model.

  • Yes-rate: the share of a party’s members voting on a roll call who voted yes.
  • Gap: the difference between the Democratic and Republican yes-rates (0 = voted alike, 1 = strict party line). Bipartisan means a gap under 0.25; party-line means a gap over 0.8.
  • Votes on bills are final-passage, fast-track (suspension) and amendment votes. Procedural votes, such as the rule that sets up debate, are left out of the issue analysis. An amendment takes its bill’s policy area.
  • With the other party: on votes where the two parties’ majorities voted opposite ways (317 votes, procedural rule votes excluded), the share where a member sided with the other party’s majority.
  • Delegates from DC and the territories are excluded from the comparisons, because they can vote on amendments but not on final passage.

Limits.

  • Each bill gets only one policy area, so a broad bill sits under one heading.
  • The data-center finding rests on a single bill. The bill’s official summary is the only place that names data centers; no subject term does.
  • Caucus labels come from Wikipedia’s current member lists (retrieved 2026-10-17). The Freedom Caucus doesn’t publish its roster.

Code and data: github.com/godot107/ideology-clusters (scripts/fetch_bills.py, scripts/issues.py, app/).

These are public officials’ public votes, described the same way for every member of both parties. This post doesn’t endorse any candidate or party.
